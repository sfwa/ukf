Unscented Kalman Filter

A small aircraft with rotors cannot fly unless it knows which way is up. It also needs to know how fast it moves, and where it is. It cannot measure any of these things directly.

It has sensors, and each sensor measures something else, with some error. A filter is the software that turns those readings into an estimate of what the aircraft needs to know. This article explains one kind of filter, the unscented Kalman filter, from the start.

Here is a real flight, played back. A filter of this kind works out which way the aircraft is turned. It runs in your browser, on the sensor readings from the flight. Drag the figure to turn the view.

The filter is not perfect. Over this flight its tilt is wrong by 0.86 degrees. (That is the root mean square error, the usual measure of a typical error.) It is a good result for a filter that never sees the ground, the walls or a satellite.

The figures use three colours, always with the same meaning. Blue is what the filter believes. Orange is what a sensor reads. Green is what a model predicts.

We start with one sensor and one number. Then we add motion, then a second number, then curves. After that we take the real filter apart.

A reading is not the truth

A quadcopter hovers one metre above the floor. A sensor on its underside measures the height again and again. The quadcopter does not move, but the readings do:

No sensor is exact. Each reading is the true height plus a small error, and the error is different every time. One reading alone does not tell you the height. It tells you roughly where the height is.

After many readings a pattern appears. Most readings are close to the truth and few are far from it. The bars grow into the shape of a bell curve.

The width of the bell has a name: the spread. About two readings in three fall within one spread of the truth. Almost all fall within three. A good sensor has a small spread. Move the slider to make the sensor better or worse.

A bell curve needs only two numbers: where its middle is, and its spread. A filter keeps these two numbers for everything it estimates. The middle is its estimate. The spread says how sure it is, and we call it the uncertainty of the estimate.

Two estimates are better than one

Suppose the filter already has an estimate of where the quadcopter is along a line. Then a sensor gives a reading of the same thing. The two do not agree. Which one is right?

Neither is right, but both hold information. Each is a bell curve, and you can combine the two into one:

The combined estimate lies between the two. It is nearer to the one with the smaller spread, because that one is more sure. Give the filter and the estimate moves almost all the way to the reading. Give it and the estimate stays near the prediction.

How far the estimate moves towards the reading is called the gain. A gain of 0 ignores the reading. A gain of 1 believes it completely. The filter works the gain out from the two spreads:

gain = p² / (p² + r²)

Here p is the spread of the prediction and r is the spread of the reading. Nobody chooses the gain by hand. It follows from how sure each side is.

Look at the width of the combined bell. It is always narrower than both of the others. Even a poor reading makes the estimate a little more sure.

Things move

The quadcopter does not stay still while the filter waits for the next reading. So the filter needs a rule that says where the quadcopter will be a moment later. This rule is called the process model.

A simple process model is: the new position is the old position plus speed times time. But the model is never exactly right. A gust of wind pushes the quadcopter, or the speed was a little wrong. The longer the filter predicts without a reading, the less it knows:

The prediction moves with the quadcopter, and it gets wider. The amount by which the model can be wrong is called the model noise. With more model noise the bell widens faster. Change it with the slider.

Predict, then correct

Now put the two halves together. The filter predicts, and its uncertainty grows. A reading arrives, the filter combines it with the prediction, and its uncertainty shrinks. Then it predicts again. This loop is a Kalman filter. Rudolf Kalman published it in 1960.

The lower plot shows the loop as a row of saw teeth. Each tooth rises while the filter predicts and drops when a reading arrives.

Between readings, an accelerometer tells this filter how the speed changes. Switch the accelerometer off and the filter must assume that the speed stays the same. Its predictions get worse, and so does its estimate.

The estimate is closer to the truth than the readings are. The filter does not know the truth. It gets there by weighing each new reading against everything it has seen before.

The band is the filter's own statement of how wrong it may be. If the filter is set up well, the truth stays inside the band nearly all the time. for a few seconds and the band grows, because the filter knows that it is only predicting.

What a reading says about other things

The filter in that figure also estimated the speed of the quadcopter. But the sensor measured only position. Where did the speed come from?

At the start the filter knows the position and the speed only roughly. The two have nothing to do with each other. The cloud of dots is upright.

Now . Every dot moves to the right by its own speed. The fast dots move further than the slow dots, so the cloud leans over. Position and speed are now linked: if the quadcopter is far to the right, it must be one of the fast ones.

Then . The reading rules out the dots that are far from it. The dots that remain all have nearly the same speed. A reading of position has told the filter the speed.

A Kalman filter does not keep the dots. It keeps the ellipse around them: its middle, how wide it is in each direction, and how much it leans. The numbers that describe the width and the lean are called the covariance. The lean is what lets a reading of one thing correct an estimate of another. The real filter below depends on this.

Curves

So far every rule was a straight line. Position plus speed times time is a straight line. When you push a bell curve through a straight line, you get another bell curve, and the Kalman filter's arithmetic is exact.

An aircraft is full of angles, and angles bring curves. Here is one. A quadcopter two metres above the floor looks at a marker on the floor. Its camera gives the angle between straight down and the marker. The distance to the marker along the floor is the height times the tangent of that angle.

The angle is uncertain by a few degrees. What does the filter then know about the distance? The grey bars give the true answer, and it is not a bell curve. It has a long tail towards large distances, because the curve gets steeper there.

The filter still wants a middle and a spread. One old way to get them is to replace the curve with a straight line that touches it at the middle angle. This is what an extended Kalman filter does. It is cheap, but the answer is wrong when the bell is wide. The middle is too low and the spread is too small.

The unscented Kalman filter does something else. It picks a few test values of the angle, called sigma points: one in the middle and one on each side. It puts each one through the real curve. Then it takes the middle and the spread of the three results.

Three points are very few, but they are well placed. Their answer is close to the truth. Make the bell and both methods agree, because a short piece of any curve is nearly straight. Make it and the straight line fails first.

The sigma points have a second advantage. The filter only needs to run the rule itself, never to work out its slope. For a rule as long as the motion of an aircraft, that removes a lot of work and a lot of mistakes.

Sigma points in more dimensions

A real filter estimates many numbers at once. The sigma points then sit around an ellipse, like the one in the figure with the dots. Here is an example with two numbers. A ground station measures the distance to a quadcopter, and the direction in which it lies. Where is the quadcopter on the map?

The direction is less certain than the distance, so on the map the belief bends into an arc. The sigma points follow the arc. The ellipse they give has its middle pulled back towards the station, where the middle of the arc really is. The straight-line method puts its middle too far out. to see the difference grow.

The recipe is the same for any number of dimensions:

  1. Put one sigma point at the middle of the belief.
  2. For each number in the state, put two more points on the ellipse, one on each side of the middle.
  3. Put every point through the rule.
  4. Take the weighted middle of the results, and their weighted spread.

Two numbers need five points. The filter in this article estimates 9 numbers, so it uses 19 points. Each point sits 3.46 spreads from the middle. The middle point has a weight of 1/4 and each of the others has 1/24.

Which way is up

The first thing the aircraft must know is its attitude: how it is turned. It helps to split the attitude in two. The tilt says how far the aircraft leans away from upright. The heading says which way its nose points.

The filter stores the attitude as four numbers called a quaternion. The details are not needed here. What matters is that a small error in attitude takes only three numbers: a small turn about each of three axes. Two of them are tilt and one is heading.

Gravity always points down, so a filter can find the tilt. Nothing in this aircraft points north. A gyroscope and an accelerometer cannot see the heading at all. The filter can only follow how the heading changes, and its uncertainty about the heading can only grow.

In the flight at the top of the page, the filter's tilt uncertainty is about 0.5 degrees. Its heading uncertainty is about 4 degrees halfway through, and still rising.

The filter must stop that rise at some point. Sigma points sit a fixed number of spreads from the middle. With a very large heading spread, the heading sigma points would reach half a turn away. The arithmetic for small turns would then fail. So this filter never lets its heading uncertainty pass 17 degrees.

The sensors

The aircraft in the recordings carries a small unit with two sensors, a gyroscope and an accelerometer. Each gives 200 readings every second. Together they are called an IMU.

The gyroscope

The gyroscope measures how fast the aircraft turns. Add up its readings and you get the attitude. That works for a second or two. Then a small fault in the sensor takes over.

At the start of the recording the aircraft stands still. Its gyroscope should read zero. It reads a steady turn of 4.6 degrees per second. This constant error is called the bias.

Add up the gyroscope readings and you add up the bias too. After the gyroscope alone says that the aircraft leans over by 50 degrees. At that moment it has only just taken off.

The filter treats the bias as three more numbers to estimate, one for each axis. Its state is now complete: attitude, velocity and gyroscope bias.

The lower plot shows how the filter finds the bias. When the readings have been quiet for a second and a half, the filter decides that the aircraft is at rest. An aircraft at rest does not turn, so whatever the gyroscope reads is bias. The estimate jumps to the right value and its uncertainty collapses.

The accelerometer

The accelerometer is harder to understand. People often say that it measures gravity. It does not. It measures every push on the aircraft except gravity.

. Gravity pulls it down and your hand pushes it up. The accelerometer feels only your hand. That push points straight up, so the reading shows where up is. Tilt the aircraft and the reading shows the tilt. The accelerometer works as a spirit level.

Now . Nothing holds it up except its rotors. Their thrust always points along the aircraft's own axis, whichever way the aircraft is tilted. So the accelerometer reads nearly the same thing at every tilt. In flight it is no use as a spirit level.

The recording shows this clearly:

In flight the aircraft leans by up to 31 degrees. The accelerometer never shows more than 4. A second recording begins with the aircraft . There the accelerometer follows the true tilt. It does not follow exactly, because a moving hand also pushes sideways.

Rotor drag

If you look again at the aircraft in flight, there is a second, small orange arrow. When a rotor moves sideways through the air, the air pushes back. This is rotor drag. It is weak, but the accelerometer feels it.

Rotor drag grows with speed, and it points against the motion. For speeds like those in this flight, a simple rule describes it well:

sideways reading = − drag rate × sideways speed

Sideways means across the rotors, at a right angle to the thrust. So in flight the accelerometer is not a spirit level. It is a speedometer.

The recording lets us check the rule. The motion capture system gives the true speed of the aircraft, and the accelerometer gives its reading:

The dots lie along a line through the centre, as the rule says. They do not lie exactly on it. The filter uses a drag rate of 0.25 per second for every recording. The line that fits this recording best has a slope of 0.18. The rule is rough, and the filter's settings say so: they give the rule a generous spread.

This idea comes from a paper by Leishman, Macdonald, Beard and McLain, listed at the end.

The process model

The filter now has everything it needs. Its state has three parts:

  • the attitude,
  • the velocity, measured along the aircraft's own axes,
  • the gyroscope bias.

The process model says how each part changes during one step of 5 thousandths of a second:

  • The attitude turns by the gyroscope reading, minus the bias.
  • The velocity changes because gravity pulls, the rotors push along their axis, and drag slows any sideways motion.
  • The bias stays as it is.

The push of the rotors comes from the accelerometer reading along the thrust axis. The drag comes from the rule above. The model does not use the sideways reading of the accelerometer. The filter keeps that reading back, to check the result.

One line in that list matters more than the others: gravity pulls. Gravity pulls straight down, but the velocity is measured along the aircraft's own axes. To know which way down is, the model must use the attitude.

So a wrong tilt gives a wrong velocity. Suppose the filter's tilt is wrong by one degree. The model then puts gravity one degree off. The sideways velocity goes wrong by 0.17 metres per second, every second.

The next figure shows this with the real filter. It takes the 19 sigma points at one moment of the flight. Each one is a complete state: an attitude, a velocity and a bias. The figure then runs each of them forward through the process model, without any readings.

The four sigma points that differ in tilt start with different velocities, and they drift further apart. to see how far. They cannot all be right, and the true velocity shows which ones are wrong.

That is the lean from the figure with the dots, in a real filter. Tilt and sideways velocity are linked. A reading of sideways velocity tells the filter which tilt was right.

Now show the sigma points that differ in . They do not separate at all. Rotor drag and gravity look the same whichever way the nose points. No reading of velocity can ever correct the heading.

What the filter reads

A filter also needs a rule that says what each sensor should read, given the state. The filter compares that with the real reading. This filter has three such rules, and it uses them at different times.

  • In flight, the sideways accelerometer reading should be the drag rate times the sideways velocity, with a minus sign.
  • When the rotors are off, the velocity should be about zero. This is not a reading from a sensor. It is an assumption, written as a reading with a generous spread. An aircraft that is carried or that stands on the ground does not move fast.
  • At rest, the gyroscope reading should be equal to the bias.

The filter must decide which rules apply, and it has only the IMU to decide with. Turning rotors shake the aircraft, and the accelerometer feels the shaking. Another recording shows this well. It starts with the aircraft carried by hand. Then it stands on the ground, and its rotors start and stop three times. Then it takes off.

The figure also shows where the model is wrong. Go to . The rotors turn, so the filter switches to the rotor drag rule. But the aircraft still stands on the ground. The rule is for free flight. The filter's speed climbs to more than two metres per second while the aircraft does not move.

The filter recovers as soon as the rotors stop. A process model is a statement about the world. When the statement is false, the filter is wrong, and it does not know.

One step, 200 times a second

Here is the whole filter, in the order in which it works. It does this for every pair of readings from the IMU.

  1. Place 19 sigma points around the estimate.
  2. Run each sigma point through the process model.
  3. Take their weighted middle as the new estimate. Take their weighted spread, add the model noise, and use that as the new uncertainty.
  4. Place 19 new sigma points. For each one, work out what the sensors should read.
  5. Compare the middle of those expected readings with the real reading.
  6. Move the estimate by the gain times the difference, and reduce the uncertainty.

Steps 1 to 3 are the prediction. Steps 4 to 6 are the correction. The gain in step 6 is the same idea as the gain for two bell curves. It is now a table of numbers, with one row for each part of the state. The lean of the covariance decides how much each part moves.

The next figure shows steps 4 to 6 in the real flight, slowed down twenty times.

Press play. The reading jumps around much more than the drag could change in so short a time. Most of that is the shaking of the rotors. The filter knows this, so it trusts each reading very little. One step corrects the tilt by a few hundredths of a degree.

But the filter takes 200 steps every second, and the small corrections add up. The shaking pushes one way and then the other, and it cancels. The drag always pushes the same way, and it stays.

One detail of the library: it does not store the covariance itself. It stores a square root of it. The results are the same, and the arithmetic is more stable.

The settings mean the following.

  • Gyroscope noise: how far the gyroscope's sum can wander in one second.
  • Rotor model noise: how wrong the rotor model may be about the change in velocity.
  • Drag reading noise: the spread of the sideways accelerometer reading.
  • Bias drift: how fast the gyroscope bias may change.
  • Heading uncertainty limit: the largest heading uncertainty the filter keeps.

And position?

This filter does not estimate where the aircraft is. That was not an oversight. An IMU cannot see position, just as it cannot see heading.

You can still try. Velocity is the rate of change of position, so you can add the velocity up over time. Sailors call this dead reckoning. The figure does it in two ways.

The raw IMU is lost at once. Two seconds after the start it is 1.1 metres from the truth. After ten seconds it is 101 metres away, in a room a few metres wide. The gyroscope bias tilts its idea of down, and gravity does the rest.

The filter does much better. After ten seconds it is about 3 metres away. But its error grows too, and it does not stop. to see.

The reason is in the figure with the dots. A reading can only correct what it is linked to. Rotor drag is linked to sideways velocity, and through it to tilt. Nothing that the IMU reads is linked to position. Every small error in velocity adds up and stays for ever.

To hold a position, the aircraft needs a reading of position from outside: a satellite receiver outdoors, or a camera indoors. The filter needs no new idea for that. Add three numbers to the state for the position, and one more rule for the new reading. The loop stays the same: predict, then correct.
